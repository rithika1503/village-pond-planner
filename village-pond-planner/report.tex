% ─── 1. Introduction ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

This report documents the design, implementation, and deployment of the \textbf{Village Pond Planner} --- a REST API that accepts an uploaded contour map (KML format), automatically derives terrain hydrology, identifies optimal pond locations, and returns a structured JSON response containing catchment geometry, pond sizing, and suitability scores.

The system is \textbf{fully general}: no hard-coded coordinates, village names, or pre-computed results are embedded. All outputs are derived algorithmically from the uploaded contour file at runtime, making the system directly extensible to any region anywhere in the world.

% ─── 3. Catchment Estimation Approach ─────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Catchment Estimation Approach}

\subsection{Overview}

The system implements a complete \textbf{D8 (Deterministic Eight-Direction)} flow routing pipeline in pure NumPy. The pipeline is entirely derived from the uploaded contour file --- no external elevation data is used.

\subsection{10-Step Pipeline}

\subsubsection{Step 1 -- KML Parsing}
The uploaded KML/KMZ file is parsed with \texttt{lxml}. Each \texttt{<Placemark>} is a contour line; elevation is read from \texttt{<name>} and coordinates from \texttt{<coordinates>}. Handles raw KML, KMZ archives, and various elevation notations.

\subsubsection{Step 2 -- DEM Interpolation}
Sparse contour points are interpolated onto a \textbf{150$\times$150 regular grid} using \texttt{scipy.interpolate.griddata} (cubic method), producing a smooth Digital Elevation Model (DEM).

\subsubsection{Step 3 -- Sink Filling (Wang \& Liu, 2006)}
Hydrological sinks are eliminated using a priority-queue spill-elevation algorithm ensuring every cell drains continuously to the boundary.

\subsubsection{Step 4 -- D8 Flow Direction}
Each cell is assigned a flow direction to the steepest downhill neighbour among 8 surrounding cells. Diagonal neighbours are penalised by $\sqrt{2}$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{slope}_{d} = \frac{\Delta z}{\Delta d}, \quad \Delta d = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{cardinal} \\ \sqrt{2} & \text{diagonal} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Step 5 -- Flow Accumulation}
A topological pass counts the number of upstream cells draining through each cell. High-accumulation cells = rivers; low-accumulation = ridges.

\subsubsection{Step 6 -- River Exclusion (Dual Mask)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mathematical mask:} Top 15\% flow-accumulation cells flagged as stream channels.
    \item \textbf{OSM mask:} Official \texttt{api.openstreetmap.org} queried for \texttt{waterway} features; returned XML parsed and rasterised with a 220\,m exclusion buffer.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Step 7 -- Candidate Identification}
Cells satisfying: slope $< 5°$, elevation $\leq$ 40th percentile, not river-excluded, are grouped into contiguous regions via BFS. Each region's best cell (max flow accumulation) is its representative. A \textbf{500\,m spatial-diversity filter} ensures spread-out candidates.

\subsubsection{Step 8 -- Outlet Snapping \& Upstream Trace}
The outlet is walked downstream to the 98th-percentile stream. BFS upstream from there collects all contributing cells --- the catchment.

\subsubsection{Step 9 -- Pond Sizing (Rational Method)}
\begin{align}
    V_{\text{runoff}} &= P \times A \times C \\
    A_{\text{pond}} &= V_{\text{runoff}} / d_{\text{pond}}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Step 10 -- Suitability Scoring}
\begin{equation}
    S = 0.40\,T + 0.40\,C + 0.20\,L
\end{equation}

\subsection{Why These Sites Are Best --- Detailed Explanations}

\subsubsection{Site \#1 --- Primary Recommendation}
\textbf{Location:} $21.2524^\circ$N, $81.2814^\circ$E \quad \textbf{Catchment: 5.08\,ha}\\
This site scores the highest terrain score (0.95) due to its extremely gentle slope of only $0.53^\circ$ and low elevation (280\,m --- in the bottom 40th percentile of the study area). It sits at a clear natural depression where flow accumulation converges, making excavation minimal and water harvesting most efficient. The 5.08\,ha catchment is compact and hydrologically coherent, meaning almost all rainfall in that sub-watershed reliably drains to this point.

\subsubsection{Site \#2 --- Largest Catchment}
\textbf{Location:} $21.2413^\circ$N, $81.2897^\circ$E \quad \textbf{Catchment: 124.59\,ha}\\
This is the most significant site in terms of water harvesting potential, with the \textbf{largest catchment of 124.59\,ha} --- over 24$\times$ more than Site \#1. The very gentle slope ($0.34^\circ$, the gentlest of all candidates) confirms it sits at a major valley convergence zone. The enormous catchment means it can collect rainfall from a very large upstream area, making it ideal for larger-scale water storage. However, its slightly lower terrain score indicates it may need more earthwork.

\subsubsection{Site \#3 --- Northern Valley}
\textbf{Location:} $21.2616^\circ$N, $81.2967^\circ$E \quad \textbf{Catchment: 8.88\,ha}\\
Located in the northern section of the study area, this site has a moderate slope ($0.92^\circ$) and a good flow accumulation score, indicating consistent sub-watershed drainage. The 8.88\,ha catchment is well-suited for a medium-scale village pond with low construction risk.

\subsubsection{Site \#4 -- Compact Efficient Site}
\textbf{Location:} $21.2558^\circ$N, $81.2897^\circ$E \quad \textbf{Catchment: 1.90\,ha}\\
While this site has a smaller catchment (1.90\,ha), its terrain score of 0.87 reflects favourable slope and elevation conditions. Suitable for a small drinking-water or kitchen-garden pond where a compact, easy-to-maintain water body is preferable.

\subsubsection{Site \#5 -- High Upstream Drainage}
\textbf{Location:} $21.2602^\circ$N, $81.2884^\circ$E \quad \textbf{Catchment: 33.90\,ha}\\
With 33.90\,ha of catchment and the highest flow accumulation value (14), this site receives drainage from a sizeable tributary sub-basin. The slightly steeper slope ($1.99^\circ$) means some embankment work will be needed, but the natural convergence of flow makes it hydrologically attractive.

\subsubsection{Sites \#6--\#10 --- Supporting Candidates}
Sites 6 through 10 represent viable alternatives across different parts of the study area. They range in catchment from 4.17\,ha to 69.54\,ha and all score $> 0.76$ on terrain metrics. Site \#7 (69.54\,ha) is particularly interesting as a secondary large-catchment option in the eastern portion of the study area. These sites ensure that if the primary recommendation is unsuitable due to land ownership or social factors, multiple geographically-spread alternatives exist.

% ─── 5. Code Extensibility ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Code Extensibility}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{New input format:} Only \texttt{kml\_parser.py} needs modification to return \texttt{(lats, lons, elevs)} arrays. All downstream code is reused as-is.
    \item \textbf{New scoring dimension:} Add a weight to \texttt{config.py} and update \texttt{scoring.py}. No router changes needed.
    \item \textbf{Raster DEM input (GeoTIFF):} Replace \texttt{contours\_to\_dem()} with a rasterio reader returning the same NumPy arrays.
    \item \textbf{Multi-village:} The API is fully stateless. Calling it $N$ times with $N$ KML files yields $N$ independent analyses.
    \item \textbf{All tunable parameters} live in \texttt{backend/app/config.py} --- grid resolution, slope threshold, stream percentile, scoring weights --- without touching business logic.
\end{itemize}
